      Chapter two

      The AI Art Studio

      Draw something on the sketchbook or pin up a drawing of your own, and Pip will tell you how our model reads it.

      Digital drawings can behave differently from the scanned paper drawings the model was trained on. The paper texture you see is decoration only. The model receives a plain white page.

      • Nothing you upload or draw is stored.
      • The image is resized to 256 × 180 pixels, the size the model was trained on.
      • The result is a drawing category, not a judgement about anyone's feelings.

      What does this score mean?

      The model ends with a single sigmoid output between 0 and 1. In our setup it is the Sadness score: above 0.5 the model predicts Sadness, otherwise Happiness. This is the same rule we used in our notebook.

      The score shows how far the drawing sits from that boundary. It is not the chance that the prediction is right. Even a score near 0 or 1 can be wrong. On our test set the model was right about 7 times out of 10.

      This is a computer-vision experiment, not a psychological or medical assessment.

      Chapter three

      Behind the Intelligence

      How five students taught a neural network to tell two kinds of drawings apart, and where it still gets confused.

      1

      The question

      Can a deep learning model distinguish children's drawings made to represent happiness from those made to represent sadness?

      And a second question: does removing colour change how well it does?

      2

      The KIDO dataset

      10,860 drawings

      Scanned paper drawings by children, each drawn to a prompt: Happiness or Sadness. Perfectly balanced: 5,430 of each.

      Train 7,382ValTest

      Validation 1,846 · Test 1,632. Split by student, so no child's drawings appear in two splits.

      3

      From scratch: CNNs

      We first built convolutional networks ourselves: stacks of filters that learn edges, then shapes, then compositions.

      Four conv blocks reached ~64% macro-F1: the models were underfitting.

      4

      Transfer learning

      MobileNetV2, pretrained on millions of everyday photos, already knows edges, textures and shapes. We reused it in two stages:

      1. Feature extraction: freeze it, train a new head.
      2. Fine-tuning: unfreeze the last 30 layers with a tiny learning rate (1e-5).
      5

      The pipeline inside this website

      1. Drawingany size
      2. Resize180 × 256 px
      3. Normalisepixels ÷ 255 → 0 to 1
      4. Rescale× 2 − 1, inside the model
      5. MobileNetV2ImageNet weights
      6. Pool + Dropoutglobal average · 0.3
      7. Sigmoidone score, 0 to 1
      8. Decision> 0.5 → Sadness

      The same preprocessing as our notebook, checked by a prediction parity test.

      6

      Six experiments · validation macro-F1

      The final model was chosen on validation data only, then evaluated once on the test set.

      9

      Limitations: read before you trust the machine

      • It predicts a category, not a feeling. Labels describe the prompt each child was given, not their emotional state.
      • Wrong about 3 times in 10. A high score does not mean the prediction is correct.
      • Trained on scanned paper. Digital sketches and phone photos may be read differently.
      • No explanations. The model does not tell us why, and we don't attribute meaning to particular colours or shapes.
      • One dataset. Results may not carry over to drawings from other places, ages or materials.
